## 1. Why precursor observations matter

Precursor data from Hubble, Euclid, Rubin, and ground-based facilities can provide:

- earlier astrometric positions and proper motions;
- high-resolution source and blend information;
- optical/infrared colors;
- long-baseline variability;
- field and detector characterization;
- preliminary event-rate constraints.

The value comes from a longer time baseline and different wavelengths/resolutions, not merely duplicate imaging.

### Why several filter systems help

Hubble, Euclid, Rubin, and Roman cover overlapping but different wavelength ranges. Together they can:

- constrain stellar temperature and extinction;
- distinguish source, lens, and blended light by color;
- extend astrometric and variability baselines;
- test whether an apparent feature is instrument-specific.

Cross-mission use requires consistent source matching, zero points, passband models, angular-resolution treatment, and observation times.

## 2. Follow-up of microlensing events

![Follow-up observations add information across time](../assets/key-concepts/follow-up.svg)

Potential follow-up goals:

- measure finite-source or parallax effects;
- cover planetary anomalies missed by one observatory;
- obtain source spectroscopy;
- resolve lens and source years later;
- constrain lens flux and host mass;
- distinguish degenerate light-curve solutions.

Large ground-based telescopes, adaptive optics, and space observatories each offer different strengths.

## 3. Simultaneous surveys

Observing one event from separated locations changes the apparent lens–source trajectory. This **space–ground parallax** can strongly constrain \(\boldsymbol{\pi}_E\).

Contemporaneous time-domain surveys also:

- fill temporal gaps;
- add optical colors;
- detect events outside Roman's field;
- extend sensitivity to different timescales;
- provide independent systematics.

Coordination requires common alerts, timestamps, astrometry, and data standards.

## 4. General Observer programs

Roman General Observer (GO) time can expand exoplanet science by:

- revisiting fields outside baseline seasons;
- adding filters;
- observing complementary Galactic environments;
- targeting follow-up fields or known systems;
- extending the period baseline for transits;
- enabling community-proposed analyses.

Any added observations change the selection function. GO science intended for demographics needs survey-like documentation.

## 5. Non-exoplanet astrophysics from GBTDS

Repeated high-resolution infrared imaging can support:

- stellar variability and pulsation;
- eclipsing binaries;
- compact-object and isolated black-hole lensing;
- stellar populations in the bulge;
- star-formation and extinction mapping;
- proper motions and Galactic dynamics;
- transients and rare variables;
- stellar flares and rotation;
- astrometric binaries.

The same pipeline decisions optimized for planets may not be optimal for every variable source, so flexible data products matter.

## 6. Solar System science

Moving objects can cross the Roman fields:

- asteroids;
- trans-Neptunian objects;
- distant small bodies;
- possible satellites or binaries.

Repeated positions constrain orbits. Shift-and-stack methods can recover objects too faint in a single exposure. Trails and moving sources are both scientific signals and potential contaminants for stellar time-series analyses.

## 7. Coronagraph technology

Roman's Coronagraph Instrument is a technology demonstration for high-contrast imaging. Key ideas:

- suppress starlight with a coronagraph;
- control wavefront errors;
- distinguish planets or disks from residual speckles;
- validate detectors, masks, deformable mirrors, and control algorithms in space.

It is scientifically and technically distinct from the wide-field GBTDS, but both contribute to the broader exoplanet program.

## 8. Toward the Habitable Worlds Observatory

The proposed **Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO)** aims to directly image and characterize potentially habitable planets around nearby stars. Roman contributes:

- high-contrast technology maturation;
- operational experience;
- detector and wavefront-control lessons;
- a statistical context for planetary systems;
- a bridge between population surveys and direct atmospheric characterization.

Microlensing and transits answer “How common are different planets?” Direct imaging and spectroscopy ask “What are nearby planets and atmospheres like?”
